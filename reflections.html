Weekly Reflections

These reflections document my Directed Fieldwork experience at the Maine Historical Society and my growth in archival processing, reference services, and professional workflows.

Week One Reflection (5 Hours)

My first week at the Maine Historical Society (MHS) was all about getting familiar with the organization and learning where I would be working throughout the summer. I started with an orientation that included a tour of the museum, the current exhibition Pathways to Freedom, the Wadsworth-Longfellow House, and the Brown Research Library.

I met Hannah, my supervisor for this Directed Fieldwork, along with several of the volunteers who give tours of the Wadsworth-Longfellow House. I also met Katie, the Research & Administrative Librarian, and learned more about her role and the work being done in the research library.

Hannah and I spent some time this first week going over the goals for my fieldwork, the schedule we would follow over the summer, and the different projects I would be working on or that are currently underway within MHS. We also talked about where I could be the most helpful, the projects I would be supporting, and the skills I would be developing throughout the summer.

I really enjoyed my first week because it gave me a better understanding of everything the Maine Historical Society does. Before starting, I mostly thought of MHS as a museum, so I was surprised to learn about the Brown Research Library and the role it plays in preserving Maine’s history and supporting researchers.

Week Two Reflection (5 Hours)

This week, I spent time shadowing staff and taking stock of the binders used by volunteers at the Wadsworth-Longfellow House. These binders serve as an important resource during house tours, so one of my main responsibilities was reviewing each one to make sure the information was accurate and up to date. I carefully went through the indexes for each room, checked that the materials matched the correct locations, and edited any outdated or incorrect information. My goal was to make sure volunteers had reliable resources to reference while giving tours and answering visitors' questions.

Working on this project showed me how much behind-the-scenes work goes into creating a positive experience for the public. Even though updating the binders seemed like a small task at first, I realized how important it is to keep information organized and consistent. If volunteers are working from outdated materials, it can affect the accuracy of the tours and the overall visitor experience. Having updated resources also helps volunteers feel more confident sharing the history of each space during their tours .

Throughout the week, I also had the opportunity to observe how staff communicated with volunteers and how much planning goes into supporting both the historic house and the research library. It gave me a better understanding of the collaborative effort required to maintain a historic site and ensure that everyone has access to the information they need.

This project reminded me that archival and library work isn't just about preserving materials. There's also a lot of thought that goes into organizing information so it's easy for volunteers, researchers, and visitors to use. Even though updating the binders seemed like a small task at first, I realized how much of a difference accurate information makes to the overall visitor experience and volunteer knowledge of the space they are showing. While the work required close attention to detail, I found it rewarding to know that my contributions would help both the volunteers and the visitors throughout the season.

Week Three Reflection (5 Hours)

This was an exciting week! I finally had the opportunity to spend more time in the Brown Research Library, where I was able to explore the collections, learn about the library's daily operations, and work alongside Katie, the Research & Administrative Librarian.

One of the first things I learned was that many of the patrons who visit the Brown Research Library are researching their family history, looking for historical maps of Maine's coastline, or exploring Maine's rich history through the library's collections. What makes the library unique is that every item in the collection is either from Maine, about Maine, or donated by a Mainer. It was interesting to see how focused the collection is and how valuable it is to researchers from both Maine and beyond.

Throughout the week, Katie introduced me to the Maine Memory Network and showed me how much work goes into maintaining and providing access to digital collections. I learned that newspapers, letters, books, maps, photographs, and many other historical materials are scanned, uploaded, indexed, and organized so they can be searched online. I was amazed by how much information is available through the platform and by the amount of work required to make these resources accessible to the public. I also learned that the Maine Memory Network includes over 300 partner organizations across the state and serves as Maine's only statewide digital museum, bringing together collections from libraries, museums, and historical societies into one searchable resource.

I spent a lot of time with Katie exploring the collections, organizing materials in the library, and learning more about the systems used to manage both the physical and digital collections. During the week, I also had the opportunity to handle archival boxes, retrieve materials from the library's storage areas, re-shelve collections, and organize materials after they had been used. These tasks helped me become more familiar with how collections are stored, cared for, and made available to researchers.

Watching Katie respond to patron requests and explain how she uses catalogs and finding aids gave me a much better understanding of reference work. It was interesting to see how the skills I've been learning in class are applied every day to locate historical materials and connect researchers with the resources they need.

One of my favorite parts of the week was talking with Katie about the library’s backlog. She was honest about the challenges of keeping up with a growing collection, and it was nice to be able to share some of the ideas and best practices I've been learning at the University of Washington. We brainstormed different ways to approach the backlog, and it was rewarding to feel like I could contribute to the conversation while also continuing to learn from her experience.That conversation made me realize how important organization and consistent processing are for making archival collections accessible over time.

By the end of the week, I felt much more comfortable working in the Brown Research Library and had a better understanding of how its collections support researchers and the community. I'm excited to start working more directly with this backlog because it will give me the chance to apply what I've been learning while gaining more hands-on archival processing experience.

Week Four Reflection (5 Hours)

This week, I started becoming more involved with the day-to-day work in the Brown Research Library and continued learning how archival collections are managed behind the scenes. As I became more comfortable with the library's systems and workflows, I was able to take on more responsibility and ask more in-depth questions about the work being done.

One area I spent more time learning about was the library's backlog and the process of organizing collections that have not yet been fully processed. It was interesting to see how much planning goes into deciding where to begin and how collections are prioritized. While every collection is different, I started to understand the importance of creating consistent organization so that materials can eventually be described, preserved, and made accessible to researchers.

Throughout the week, I continued working with Katie and learned more about how the Brown Research Library supports both in-person and online researchers. I observed how reference questions are approached, how catalogs and finding aids are used to locate materials, and how staff determine the best resources to meet a patron's request. Seeing these interactions firsthand gave me a better understanding of the reference process and how much knowledge is required to connect researchers with the information they're looking for.

One thing I appreciated this week was having the opportunity to ask questions and discuss different approaches to archival work. It was helpful to connect what I've been learning in my MLIS coursework with the work being done at MHS. I found myself recognizing concepts from class in a real-world setting, which helped reinforce what I've learned and gave me a better understanding of how archival theory translates into practice.

By the end of the week, I felt more confident navigating the library and its collections. I also felt like I was moving beyond simply observing and becoming a more active participant in the projects I would be working on throughout the rest of my fieldwork. I'm excited to continue building my archival processing, reference, and professional communication skills as I take on more hands-on responsibilities.
